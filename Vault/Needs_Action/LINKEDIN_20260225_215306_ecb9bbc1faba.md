**Pakistan is borrowing $1.2 billion to survive a crisis it barely caused.**

The IMF's third Extended Fund Facility review — now unlocking a combined $1.2 billion — includes a component that rarely makes headlines: the Resilience and Sustainability Facility (RSF). This isn't just economic bailout money. It's climate finance. And the fact that Pakistan needs it tells a story about one of the world's most severe environmental injustices.

Pakistan contributes less than 1% of global greenhouse gas emissions. Yet it consistently ranks among the top 10 most climate-vulnerable nations on earth, according to the Global Climate Risk Index. The numbers are staggering: the 2022 super-floods submerged one-third of the country, displaced 33 million people, destroyed 2 million homes, and caused over $30 billion in damages. That single climate event wiped out nearly a decade of development gains.

**The Environmental Crisis Behind the Headlines**

While economists debate debt ratios and fiscal targets, Pakistan's environmental reality is deteriorating in real time:

- **Glacial melt**: Pakistan holds more glaciers than any region outside the polar ice caps — over 7,500 glaciers in the Karakoram, Hindukush, and Himalayan ranges. Rising temperatures are accelerating glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs), threatening millions in Gilgit-Baltistan and KP. According to UNDP, over 3,000 glacial lakes have formed, with 33 at high risk of bursting.

- **Water scarcity**: Pakistan is already a water-stressed country, with per capita water availability having dropped from 5,000 cubic metres in 1947 to under 1,000 today — dangerously close to absolute scarcity thresholds defined by the UN.

- **Air pollution**: Lahore has repeatedly ranked as one of the world's most polluted cities. The annual smog season — driven by crop burning, industrial emissions, and vehicle exhaust — causes an estimated 128,000 premature deaths in Pakistan each year, per WHO data.

- **Deforestation**: Pakistan loses approximately 27,000 hectares of forest annually, contributing to increased flood risk, soil erosion, and biodiversity loss. Despite the Billion Tree Tsunami initiative showing promise, forest cover remains critically low at under 5% of total land area.

**Climate Finance Is Not Charity — It's Accountability**

The RSF component of the IMF package represents something important: a formal acknowledgment that climate vulnerability is an economic issue. Countries like Pakistan cannot achieve fiscal stability without climate resilience. Every flood season undoes infrastructure investment. Every heat wave collapses agricultural productivity. Every GLOF event displaces communities that then strain urban services.

Pakistan's Finance Minister framing the IMF review as a success is understandable from a macroeconomic standpoint. But we must ask harder questions: Is Pakistan's climate adaptation agenda embedded in how this $1.2 billion will be deployed? Are environmental conditions attached to structural reforms? Is Pakistan using this moment to build long-term climate resilience — or simply stabilising the same systems that remain fatally exposed to the next climate shock?

**The Path Forward**

Pakistan needs a climate-integrated economic policy, not parallel tracks where Finance Ministry and Climate Ministry operate independently. The RSF is a step, but meaningful climate finance requires:

1. Transparent allocation of RSF funds to verified climate adaptation projects
2. Investment in early warning systems for GLOFs and flash floods
3. Accelerated transition away from coal-fired power in the energy mix
4. National enforcement of clean air standards in major cities
5. Scaling the Billion Tree initiative with measurable biodiversity outcomes

Pakistan is not just an economic story. It is a climate story. And until that framing takes centre stage in our policy discourse, the next $1.2 billion will not be the last bailout we need.
