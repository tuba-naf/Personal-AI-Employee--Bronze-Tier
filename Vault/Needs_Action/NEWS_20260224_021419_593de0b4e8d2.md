# News Analysis — Global Problem (Pakistan Connection)

## The Third Pole is Melting: Himalayan Glaciers, Pakistan's Water Crisis, and the World's Climate Failure

---

### Summary

A landmark study published in *Nature Climate Change* in February 2026 has confirmed what climate scientists have warned for years: the glaciers of the Hindu Kush-Himalayan (HKH) mountain system — widely known as the "Third Pole" — are retreating at a rate 65% faster than in the previous decade. For Pakistan, a country with more glaciers than any non-polar nation on Earth, this is not a distant environmental statistic. It is a countdown clock on the water security of 240 million people.

---

### The Third Pole: Pakistan's Most Vital — and Most Vulnerable — Resource

Pakistan is home to **7,253 glaciers** — more than anywhere outside the Arctic and Antarctic combined. The Hindu Kush, Karakoram, and Himalayan mountain ranges form the headwaters of the Indus River system, which provides water to **36 million Pakistanis** directly and supports the agriculture that feeds the nation.

Approximately 60–70% of the Indus River's annual flow comes from glacial melt and seasonal snowmelt. Strip away the glaciers and you strip away Pakistan's water supply.

The 2026 *Nature Climate Change* study, analysing satellite glacier mass data from 2000–2025, found that the region's glaciers lost 28% more mass in the 2015–2025 decade than in the decade before. The Karakoram range — which sits almost entirely within Pakistan — showed some of the steepest declines, reversing a previously anomalous stability known as the "Karakoram anomaly."

---

### The Flood Paradox: Too Much Water, Then None

The cruel paradox of accelerating glacial melt is that it creates two opposite catastrophes simultaneously: **devastating short-term floods** and **catastrophic long-term water scarcity**.

As glaciers melt faster, they fill glacial lakes at unprecedented rates. When these lakes breach their natural ice or moraine dams, they trigger **Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs)** — walls of ice-cold water and debris that can travel at 100 km/h down narrow mountain valleys, destroying everything in their path.

Pakistan experienced **35 GLOFs in 2023 alone** — the highest number in recorded history. The 2022 "super-floods," which inundated one-third of Pakistan, were partly driven by accelerated glacial contribution to river flow. The World Bank estimated total damages at **USD 14.9 billion**, making it one of the costliest climate disasters in South Asian history.

But the same glaciers that flood Pakistan in the coming decades will eventually disappear. The International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development (ICIMOD) projects that **up to one-third of Hindu Kush-Himalayan glaciers could be lost by 2050** under a 1.5°C warming scenario — and up to two-thirds under 3°C warming (our current trajectory).

When the glaciers are gone, the Indus will carry only what the monsoon provides — and Pakistan's monsoon seasons are becoming increasingly erratic, intense when they arrive, and increasingly unpredictable in timing.

---

### Critique: The Global Climate Failure and Pakistan's Disproportionate Burden

Pakistan contributes less than **1% of global greenhouse gas emissions** — yet it consistently ranks among the world's **top 10 most climate-vulnerable nations** (Germanwatch Global Climate Risk Index, 2025).

This is the fundamental injustice at the heart of the climate crisis: the nations least responsible for causing it are bearing the most devastating consequences. The G20 nations, responsible for over 80% of cumulative global emissions, have repeatedly failed to meet their Paris Agreement commitments. Global temperatures are currently tracking toward 2.7°C above pre-industrial levels — well beyond the 1.5°C threshold that would preserve most Himalayan glaciers.

The **$100 billion per year climate finance pledge** made by wealthy nations to developing countries at Copenhagen in 2009 was finally delivered — 13 years late — in 2022. Pakistan has received a fraction of the adaptation finance it needs to build GLOF early warning systems, relocate vulnerable mountain communities, and invest in water storage infrastructure for the post-glacier future.

COP29 in Baku (2024) produced a **$300 billion per year** climate finance commitment — a tripling of the previous figure, but still far below the $1.3 trillion that developing nations' negotiators argued was needed.

---

### The Hopeful Angle: Pakistan's Adaptation Response

Despite the scale of the crisis, Pakistan is not standing still.

The **National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA)** has deployed 27 GLOF early warning systems in the Gilgit-Baltistan and Chitral regions as of early 2026, giving downstream communities up to 4 hours of advance warning before flood surges arrive. The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) has partnered with NDMA on a **PKR 8 billion GLOF-II project** covering high-risk glacial lake sites.

The **Billion Tree Tsunami** — now expanded to the **Ten Billion Tree Tsunami** — is Pakistan's flagship reforestation initiative. With over 1.5 billion trees verified as planted or protected in its first phase, the programme is rebuilding watershed forest cover that moderates water flow and reduces soil erosion during floods.

International climate finance is beginning to reach Pakistan's mountain communities. The Green Climate Fund approved a **USD 37.6 million adaptation grant** for Pakistan in 2025 to support climate-resilient mountain agriculture and improved glacial monitoring.

### Recommendations

For Pakistan to survive the Third Pole crisis, the following must be prioritised:

1. **Accelerate GLOF monitoring expansion** to all 33 high-risk glacial lakes in Gilgit-Baltistan
2. **Invest in large-scale water storage** — dams, reservoirs, and aquifer recharge — to capture glacial runoff before it disappears
3. **Pursue climate loss and damage compensation** through international courts and climate negotiations, building on Pakistan's leadership at COP27
4. **Integrate glacial risk into national urban and agricultural planning** — the lower Indus basin must prepare for reduced long-term water flows
5. **Lead a regional South Asian Glacier Monitoring Consortium** with India, China, Afghanistan, and Nepal — the Himalayas do not recognise national borders

### Conclusion

The Hindu Kush-Himalayan glaciers are not just ice. They are Pakistan's water bank, built over thousands of years of snowfall and cold. Climate change is making a catastrophic withdrawal from that bank — one the world's largest emitters refuse to fully reckon with.

Pakistan cannot stop the global temperature rise alone. But it can — and must — lead the conversation at international climate forums, invest aggressively in adaptation, and build the resilience its people deserve.

The Third Pole is melting. The alarm bells are deafening. The world must listen.
